2026 Auto Tariffs · Origin Analysis

The badge on the hood doesn’t tell you how American a car is.

Every automaker is required by law to report how much of each model’s parts value comes from the U.S. and Canada. Ranked across 344 model-year 2026 vehicles, the “most American” cars turn out not to be the American brands. The tariff follows the supply chain, not the logo.

00 In brief

What 344 cars reveal about the tariff

One public filing, every 2026 model, one question: when the tariff hits, who actually pays for it? Five findings.

  1. The most American-made cars wear foreign badges. A Volkswagen, two Hondas, a Tesla and a Kia top the domestic-parts ranking, each one ahead of the flagship models from Ford, GM and Chrysler.
  2. Assembly location decides sourcing, not the brand. A U.S.-built car averages 46% foreign parts, a Mexico-built one 80%, anything overseas 95% or more. Fix the plant and the badge stops mattering.
  3. Electrification split the industry three ways. Some brands import the electric trim whole and watch it collapse. Toyota builds hybrids on the same U.S. line and holds steady. GM and Genesis made the EV the more American choice.
  4. “U.S.-built” is not tariff-safe. Among American-assembled cars carrying foreign parts, Mexico supplies more of them than Japan, Korea, China and Germany combined.
  5. The tariff has not reached the sticker. New-vehicle prices held inside a 1.4% band for two and a half years. Automakers absorbed the cost. The hit to buyers, if it comes, is still ahead.

This isolates the real driver. Changing the badge (down a column) barely shifts the number; changing the plant (across a row) moves it 30 to 50 points. Foreign brands look more American mainly because they assemble more of their lineup in U.S. plants, while domestic brands send their smaller, thin-margin models to Mexico. The badge is a marketing choice; the plant is where tariff exposure is set.

10 Methodology & sources

How this was built, and where the numbers come from

Everything here traces to public data. This lays out the sources, what each metric means, and the limits worth knowing before you cite it.

Data sources

  • Parts sourcing and assembly: NHTSA Part 583 AALA reports, model years 2024, 2025 and 2026. Automakers are required by law to file these.
  • Vehicle prices: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics via FRED, new vehicles (CUSR0000SETA01) and used cars and trucks (CUSR0000SETA02), seasonally adjusted.
  • Per-model price increases: third-party estimates from Cox Automotive, Kelley Blue Book and S&P Global Mobility (2026).
  • Tariff policy and rates: Congressional Research Service, Section 232 Automotive Tariffs: Issues for Congress (IN12545, May 2026), a nonpartisan Library of Congress source.

What the numbers mean

  • The U.S./Canada parts figure is the share of a vehicle’s parts value that comes from the U.S. and Canada, combined, as the labeling law defines it. It is a share of value, not a count of parts.
  • It excludes final assembly labor, profit and shipping, and is rounded to the nearest 5%.
  • Import exposure is simply the inverse: 100 minus the U.S./Canada figure. Higher means more of the value is foreign, so more of it a tariff can touch.
  • Final assembly is where the car is put together, which often differs from where its parts come from.
  • Close to the real tariff base. For USMCA-compliant vehicles, the 25% tariff is charged only on the non-U.S. parts, so the foreign-parts share here approximates the value the tariff actually falls on, not just a loose proxy.

How the trend was built

  • To compare years fairly, the trend tracks a fixed basket of 15 top-selling nameplates rather than a moving average of a changing lineup.
  • Each nameplate is held to a single volume trim across all three years, so the line reflects real sourcing change, not a trim swap.
  • The Tucson and Model 3 are excluded from the trend because their U.S. production or trim mix changed between years, which would distort a like-for-like line.

Caveats

  • Trim averaging. Where one figure is shown per model elsewhere, it averages across trims, so read those as ranges.
  • Price panel. The per-model dollar figures are third-party estimates that move over time, shown to sanity-check the ranking, not derived from the parts data.
  • Snapshot. The main ranking is model year 2026. The parts data updates once a year; the price index updates monthly.
  • No part-level detail. The filing reports one blended U.S./Canada figure per vehicle, not a part-by-part or U.S.-versus-Canada breakdown.
  • Rates now vary by origin. The tariff began as a flat 25% in April 2025, but partner deals since set different totals (about 15% for the EU, Japan and South Korea, 10% for the UK), with some still in flux. This ranking treats all foreign parts alike, so it maps to exposure, not to any single tariff rate.
